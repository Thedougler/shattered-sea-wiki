# One-Shot Modular Method (MMOS)

> Agent use: Read before designing a one-shot or session that must survive time cuts.

## Fast Rules

- Pre-communicate premise and goal before play.
- Build removable encounters so time cuts do not break the session.
- Use two paths, one key, modular encounters, showdown, and ending.
- Keep the hook concrete enough to start immediately.


A 7-step framework (Steps 0–6) by Pointy Hat for writing one-shots completable in a single session. Core design principle: **modularity** — most encounters can be removed without breaking the structure if the session runs short.

## Step 0: Above Table (Pre-Game)

Do these before play begins to save session time:

1. Tell players the quest in advance.
2. Have players describe their characters to each other (skip in-game introductions).
3. Communicate that the goal is to finish in one session.

Pre-communicating the quest sets player expectations, keeps them on-scope, and prevents accidental sandbox drift.

## Step 1: The Inciting Quest

The one-shot begins after the players have accepted the quest. The opening scene can be immediately post-briefing, at the quest location, or en route (if there's a travel encounter worth playing).

> Examples: Find the Amulet, Defeat the Demon, Win the Race, Escort the Duke, Survive the Night, Unmask the Killer, Escape the Labyrinth.

## Step 2: The Two Paths

Every MMOS adventure offers players two routes to the conclusion.

| Path | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Path of Least Resistance** | The most obvious route — goes directly to completing the quest. |
| **Path Least Traveled On** | The meandering route — leads to extra encounters and may not obviously connect to the quest. Ends with finding the Key. |

The choice between paths is itself a meaningful player decision.

> Example: Quest is to find a locket in the manor basement. Path of Least Resistance = go straight to the basement. Path Least Traveled On = visit the ghost maid's room on the top floor first.

## Step 3: The Key

The Key is a reward for taking the Path Least Traveled On. It can be a literal object or information that recontextualizes the quest, and it unlocks Ending B.

> Examples: The Amulet is Cursed, the Demon is Nice, the Race is Rigged, the Duke is Dead, the Players Cannot Survive, There is More than One Killer, the Labyrinth Has Two Levels.

## Step 4: The Encounters

Encounters are the bulk of the adventure. **Most must be modular** — easy to skip without breaking the one-shot. Design encounters to either stand alone or include a bypass that delivers whatever the party needs to continue.

**Recommended minimum load:**

| Type | Count |
|------|-------|
| Combat Encounters | 2 |
| Roleplay Encounter | 1 |
| Exploration Challenge or Puzzle | 1 |

The majority of encounters sit on the Path of Least Resistance. The Path Least Traveled On needs at least one additional encounter.

> Examples: A Safe Locked by a Puzzle, a Battle with a Diabolist, a Racing Skill Challenge, an Ambush, a Chase Scene, an Interrogation, a Trapped Battlefield.

## Step 5: The Showdown

The climax — the only non-modular encounter. Cannot be skipped. Usually a boss battle but can be any encounter type (tense roleplay, timed puzzle, etc.).

If the Key changes the circumstances of the showdown, keep the mechanical core the same and change only the flavor to minimize prep.

> Examples: A Bidding War for the Amulet, a Bombastic Battle Against a Demon, a Fight On Top of a Moving Vehicle, a Series of Waves of Enemies, a Trial to Unmask the Killer, a Mad Dash Out of a Crumbling Structure.

## Step 5: The Endings

Prepare two endings to honor player choice.

| Ending | Trigger | Description |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **Ending A (Adequate)** | No Key required | Players used the Path of Least Resistance. Quest completed in the most obvious way. Rare in practice. |
| **Ending B (Ballsy)** | Key required | Players explored the Path Least Traveled On. The Key must visibly affect the outcome so players feel their choices mattered. |

Additional branch choices can be added at the ending since consequences don't need to be accounted for long-term.

## Step 6: The Hook

An implied continuation — suggests these characters have further adventures ahead. Make hooks dependent on player choices during the session to reinforce that the world responds to their actions. Hooks can also seed a full campaign if the group wants to continue.

> Examples: Create a New Amulet, Go to Hell, Qualify for the World Race, Find the Duke's Widow, Organize a Survival Game, Travel to Inform the Victim's Family, Explore the Other Three Labyrinths.

## Design Principles

- Modularity is the primary structural goal — most encounters must be skippable.
- Player choice must be real: the two paths and the two endings only matter if both exist and are meaningfully different.
- The Key is the mechanism that makes Ending B feel earned rather than arbitrary.
- Pre-game communication (Step 0) is as important as any in-session design choice.
