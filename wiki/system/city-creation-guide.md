# City Creation Guide (Theme Park Method)

> Agent use: Read before creating a city, port, district, or location hub meant for play.

## Fast Rules

- Choose one legible theme before adding districts or lore.
- Design attractions players can interact with, not a lore museum.
- Each attraction needs activity, conflict, reward, and sensory identity.
- History matters only where it changes play at the table.

## Core Philosophy

A city is a space players want to explore, not a lore classroom or backdrop for one plot beat. Design from a gameplay-first perspective. Think of a city as a theme park: a distinct identity with a variety of activities tailored to what the players at this specific table enjoy.

## Step 1: Choose a Theme

The theme is the city's visual and conceptual throughline. Everything should feel like it belongs to the same world defined by it.

**Good theme:** immediately visual, broad enough to support diverse content, specific enough to give distinct identity.

**Bad theme:** abstract or emotional ("Hope"), so narrow it supports only one content type ("Blue"), or generic.

Ask: what is this city *built on*, *obsessed with*, or *defined by*? The answer should be visible in architecture, economy, social hierarchy, and daily life.

**Theme examples:**

- *Undeath* — class distinctions extend into death; necromancy is the most valued school; true undeath is an honor; ruled by a council of liches
- *Hell* — grew from a paladin keep beside an uncloseable portal; trade with devils became a full economy; tiefling/cambion population from centuries of mingling
- *Underwater* — larger below the surface than above; nobility lives underwater; the working class lives in the sun
- *Holy* — theocracy after a cataclysm; Church monopolizes healing; heresy is treason
- *Art* — soft power as statecraft; bardic colleges dwarf wizarding ones; fame is currency

## Step 2: Build Attractions

Attractions are the things players can *do* in the city. Identify what players at this table enjoy first: roleplay, combat, puzzles, competitions, exploration, intrigue, stealth, loot.

**An attraction is most valuable when it appeals to more than one player simultaneously.**

| Category | Core Concept | Player Appeal |
|---|---|---|
| Fighting Contest | Face teams for a prize | Combat, loot |
| Infiltration | Enter a restricted area undetected | Stealth, intrigue |
| Ball / Social Event | Convince an attendee to align with party | Roleplay, intrigue |
| Competition | Contest of skill for a prize | Skill builds, loot |
| Performance | Earn money or get noticed | Performance, roleplay |
| Test of Mettle | Prove combat prowess for a job or ally | Combat |
| Dispatching an Enemy | Bring a dangerous resident down | Combat, intrigue, planning |
| Siege | Reinforce city defenses | Combat, planning |
| Explore Uncharted Territory | Sewers, ruins, forbidden areas | Exploration, puzzles, combat |
| Gather Clues | Collect scattered evidence | Exploration, intrigue, roleplay |

Attractions can be combined. Infiltration + Ball = some players mingle while others run a stealth mission simultaneously.

Adapt every attraction to the theme. A Fighting Contest in an Art city is a battle of the bands; in a Hell city it's a duel run by a Pit Fiend.

Failure states should redirect the story or open new attractions — not dead-end.

## Design Checklist

- Theme is immediately visual and distinct
- Theme is present in architecture, economy, culture, and NPCs
- At least one attraction per player preference at this table
- Each attraction adapted to fit the theme
- Some attractions overlap in appeal (multiple players engaged simultaneously)
- The city has something worth returning to after main plot beats resolve
